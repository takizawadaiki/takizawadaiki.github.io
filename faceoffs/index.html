What makes a player elite at faceoffs?

This page rates every NHL faceoff man on the same scale, tests how much size, age and handedness explain, and asks a question I had been wondering about: could a very small player with great hands and timing earn a roster spot as a designated faceoff man?

      What to keep in mind

      • Reflexes are not measured. There is no public data on reaction time or hand speed. What the data can show is how much of faceoff skill is left over once size, age and handedness are accounted for. That remainder covers technique, timing, strength, cheating the drop and help from wingers, all mixed together.
      • Coaches choose who takes draws. Small or weak faceoff men are moved to the wing, so the players in this sample are the ones who were good enough to keep the job. The link between weight and skill among them may understate or overstate what would happen to a player picked at random.
      • No one in the sample is truly tiny. The lightest player with 500 faceoffs weighs 165 pounds. Anything the calculator says below that weight is the trend line carried past the data.
      • The value of a win covers 30 seconds. It counts shots and goals soon after the draw. It misses slower effects such as a line change, a clean breakout or time killed on a penalty, and it treats every draw in a zone alike, whatever the score or time left.
      • A faceoff is credited to one player. The league records a winner and a loser, but wingers and defencemen often win the loose puck. Ratings include that help.
      • Listed weights are rough. Heights and weights are the league’s listed figures from a player’s most recent season.
